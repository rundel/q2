Use this skill to provide writing advice to users and guidance on how to "improve writing in the small".
This skill is based on George Gopen's "Sense of Structure" and "Science of Scientific Writing".
The text below is summary of the contents of the book.

It explains the principles that you can use to improve your own writing.

## The Sense of Structure

Word choice is only about 15% of the matter; the other 85% is all structure.
Good style understands the relationship between the _substance_ of the sentence (what you want to say) and its _structure_ (how the words are put together).
Readers have relatively fixed experiences of where in the structure of an English sentence to look for the arrival of certain kinds of substance.

There are no set patterns you should follow.
But it is important to recognize that you, as a writer, control the structural placement of any and all of your information.

## Reader expectations at the sentence level

### Readers of English expect that the action of a sentence will be articulated by its verb.

The "action of a sentence" need not be a strinkingly active action.
It means simply "what is going on" in the sentence.
No verb is "strong" or "weak" by itself. 
A verb is strong if its apparent meaning is the focal point for what is going on in the sentence as a whole.
Conversely, a verb is weak if its apparent meaning has little or nothing to do with what is going on in the sentence as a whole.

Example:

- **Weak verb** What _would be_ the student reaction _accorded to_ the imposition of such a requirement?
- **Strong verb** How would students _react_ to such a requirement?

The second sentence is easier to read than the first sentence for two reasons:

1. We all believe more firmly that "react" was the main action
2. It took us less effort to come to that conclusion because the action was stated _where_ we expected to find it -- in the verb.

Well-crafted sentences have shape and flow: the sense of leaning forward to the verb and being rewarded there with that which we were seeking.

### Readers of English expect that a clause will tell the story of whoever or whatever shows up first.

The reading mind is constantlytrying to synthesize words and concepts _as they arrive_.
The order of that arrival therefore will significantly affect that act of synthesis.

### A multiclause sentence tells the story of whoever or whatever shows up in the sentence's main clause.

### Readers of English expect that the arrival of the grammatical subject will be followed almost immediately by the arrival of the verb.

A sentence that subverts this expectation will confuse readers.
Any information that intervenes between a subject and its verb tends to be read as an interruption.
Interruptions might be useful when used intentionally: that _can_ be the intended substance of the sentence as expressed in its structure.
But it becomes a major problem when the information that lives in the interruptive location with the "less important" sign on it actually turns out to be the most important information.

Many of our best stylists of English are skillful expectation violators; but in order to violate expectations effectively, you have to fulfill them most of the time, so that the violation is salient and the intention is apparent.

### Save the best for last

Coarsely,

1. one remembers best the ends of things
2. one remembers next best the beginnings of things
3. one remembers not nearly as well that which came in the middle

## The Stress position

The expectation of a sentence's closure produces just enough sense of arrival and fruition to create a heightened state of emphasis; it also creates a structural location called the _Stress position_.

The Stress position is the most important part of an English sentence; its existence gives us a mechanism to control the expectations of the readers.

The Stress position is produced by the act of the sentence running downhill towards its resting place. It is produced by the grammatical structure of a sentence reaching its resolution. Every moment of full syntactic closure is a Stress position.

### Readers of English expect that the material most to be emphasized in the sentence will appear at a moment of full syntactic closure, called a Stress position.

### Readers of English expect that every Stress position will be filled with material intended to receive significant emphasis.

Examples:

1. Jack has fallen in love with Jill because she completely understands his passion for raising fox terriers.
2. Because she completely understands his passion for raising fox terriers, Jack has fallen in love with Jill.

The most probable reading of the first sentence is the one where Jack falling in love with Jill is a given, and the intended new information is Jack's passion for raising fox terriers.
The second sentence, on the other hand, is structured to assume Jack's fox terrier passion is the context to introduce his falling in love.

## Reasons to rewrite sentences

- A sentence fails to live up to its promise if its completely devoid of stressworthy information.
- A sentence is too short when it has no viable candidate for the Stress position.
- A sentence is too long when it has more viable candidates for Stress positions than it has Stress positions.

Most of the time,

- Ensure that everything worthy of special emphasis is located in a Stress position
- Ensure that every Stress position is filled with something stressworthy

### Be restrained about artificial emphasis markers

You may find yourself tempted to use artificial emphasis (`**`, `_`, etc) to indicate the importance of the importance of the words you wish your reader to notice most.
When you feel this urge, you're probably recognizing that your sentence structure is failing to invoke the kinds of emphases you want.

It is important to know (1) when to resist this temptation, and (2) under what conditions the indications will serve you well.

1. When possible, relocate the tempting word or passage to the Stress position
2. Artificial emphasis can highlight words and clauses that, because of their syntactic functions, cannot easily be transferred to the Stress position. This is the case especially for pronouns and adjectives, words that are difficult to get to the sentence's end position.

Example: "It matters not _how_ you deliver this message, but only _that_ you deliver it."

### Comparative Weights and their uses

A sentence weighs more than a clause. An independent clause weighs more than a dependent clause, which ways more than a phrase. 

The following sentence seems unartful and clumsy: "I went downtown yesterday, and I had a car accident". In this sentence, the two clauses are equally balanced. The car accident gets the Stress position and therefore wins the "Is everybody looking at me?" contest, but the independent clauses are reason enough to throw readers off balance.

When context information is available, that should be used to guide the structure of the sentence. What if the motorist had been warned _not_ to go downtown yesterday? Then the following structure becomes an improvement: "When I had that car accident yesterday, I was downtown". Note how even though the subordinate clause is longer than the independent clause, the latter still holds sway.

## Motions in multiple sentences

### The beginning of the sentence is the "Topic position"

There are two moments in the reading process, which occur over and over again, that are of paramount importance to both writer and reader: they are the beginning and the ends of sentences.
We have already looked at the ends of sentences, which we have been calling the Stress position.
But the beginning of the sentence is also important, although in different ways. That structural location will be called the "Topic position".

The Topic position extends through the first few words of the sentence, up to an including the grammatical subject.
We expect the Topic position to tell us "whose story" the sentence is going to be.
But there's a second expectation of similar importance: the Topic position is where readers expect a sentence to be linked to the one immediately preceding it.

If a writer takes care of this concern on a regular basis, readers are able to follow the progression of thought from sentence to sentence without stumbling. If the writer fails to do this on a regular basis, the reader is constantly being mystified, hampered, and hobbled.

### Readers expect the material at the beginning of a sentence to provide a connection backward to the previous sentence.

Example:

- **Bad** 

  > The assignment of supplemental readings -- even if those readings were referred to in texts already approved by the official curriculum, were clearly germane to the subject in general, and could be demonstrated to be related to the core assignments -- would also require the School Board's permission

- **Better**

  > The School Board's permission would also be required before teachers would be allowed to assign supplemental readings -- even if those
  readings were referred to in works already approved by the official curriculum, were clearly germane to the subject in general, and could
  be demonstrated to be related to the core assignments.

Note how the revision is longer than the original it replaces, yet most readers would find it easier to follow and interpret.

### Readers expect the material at the beginning of a sentence to contextualize them for the new and important material to be presented later in the sentence.

### Readers form that context by answering two questions: (1) Whose story is this? and (2) How does this sentence link backward to the sentence before it?

## The Various Functions of the Stress Position

### The Herald Stress Position: The Forward Lean

The Herald Stress position produces a forward lean. It heralds the arrival of something new and important.
When you use the Stress position to herald the arrival and promise the continuance of something interesting -- and then you fulfill the promise and make that herald the "whose story" of the next sentence -- you create a structural movement we will call Topic Changing.

Bad example of misusing the Herald Stress position

- **bad**

  > Up to the time of Darwin, the only biologist to develop a theory of organic evolution in extensive fashion was Lamarck.
  > The last chapter demonstrated how much a failure was this scientific theory.
  > Darwin had read as background the writings that will be taken up here, those of such popularizers as Erasmus Darwin and Robert Chambers.
  > The development of evolutionary theory by the philosophers was apparently unknown to Darwin

  Every individual sentence sounds reasonably respectable here; but the lack of fulfillment of any promise made by a Herald Stress position damages both cohesion and coherence.

- **better**

  In the following quote, we use artificial emphasis to highlight both the occupants of the Herald Stress positions and the Topic position's backwards link. When using Topic Changing to structure a paragraph, you would not actually use artificial emphasis.  

  > Up to the time of Darwin, Lamarck was the only biologist to develop _a theory of organic evolution_ in extensive fashion.
  > _Its failure_ as a scientific theory was discussed _in the last chapter_. 
  > The writings that will be taken up _here_ as a background to the development of Darwin's theory will be those of such popularizers as Erasmus Darwin and Robert Chambers, whose work was read by _Darwin_.
  > _He_ apparently knew nothing of the _the development of evolutionary theory by the philosophers_.

  The prose moves with relative ease from sentence to sentence, establishing the connections by lighthanded lexical references and leaving us at the end with an introduction to "the development of evolutionary theory by the philosophers," which then becomes a kind of herald for the paragraph that follows.

Topic Changing is _not_ to be used as some kind of template or paradigm.
You cannot preconceive a structure for your paragraph that will lead every Stress position occupant in the next Topic position and expect to get lucky every time.
Topic Changing often works well for a sentence or three, in domino narrative situations.
It is rather rare for it to hold sway for an entire paragraph longer than three sentences

Topic Changing is nether a good thing nor a bad thing by itself.
It is highly effective when the business at hand moves from one statement to another without need for further development.
It can, however, be a disaster when the writer/reader needs to concentrate on one topic for a number of sentences in order to develop it fully.

### The Cumulative Stress Position: Increasing Thickness

If the sole function of the Stress position were to herald the arrival of the next Topic position occupant, then every paragraph would produce consistent Topic Changing. Fortunately, that is not so.
Sometimes, the occupant of the Stress position offers a new piece of information that continues the story.
To do so, the occupant of a number of consecutive Topic positions would have to remain the same.
Look at the Topic and Stress positions in the following paragraph.

- **better**

  In the following quote, we use artificial emphasis to highlight both the occupants of the Cumulative Stress positions and the Topic position's backwards link. Again, you would not actually use artificial emphasis when using this in real writing.

  > If _we_ want to surprise them with their anniversary present, we have _a number of different ways of doing it._ 
  > _We_ could leave it on the doorstep, so they would find it _when they return from dinner._
  > _We_ could leave it in their bedroom, so they will find it _just before they go to sleep._
  > _We_ could, if you think they would be in the mood for some fun, leave a letter taped to the front door with a clue where they should look next -- and leave at that place another clue -- and so forth, until they finally find it _as a result of a long scavenger hunt_.

  When this pattern asserts itself for two or more sentences in a row, we call it _Topic Stringing_.
  A string of the same thing appears in the same consecutive Topic positions, with the new additions to this cumulative story appearing in the Stress positions.

Like Topic Changing, Topic Stringing is neither good nor bad by itself.
"Using it well" means using it when it fits the needs ot the development of the argument.
Both are ways to connect the control of the reader's movement through the paragraph to the control of the reader's thought process.

### The Final Stress Position: Bring It All to an End

The Stress position can also be used at the end of things to bring everything to a final end.
Here is an example from the well-known essayist Joan Didion.
It brings her essay called "Quiet Days in Malibu" to a resting place.
She and her family had taken a trip to revisti a home in which they had once lived and which had almost been destroyed by a California forest fire.
The 14-page essay ends like this:

> After I said goodby to Amando Vazquez, my husband and daughter and I went to look at the house on the Pacific Coast Highway in which we had lived for seven years.
> The fire had come to within 125 feet of the property, then stopped or turned or been beaten back, it was hard to tell which.
> In any case, it was no longer our house.

## The Shapes of the English Paragraph

The Topic Sentence paragraph structure is a Procrustean bed.
It matters not what size or shape the bulk of your thought might be; the Topic Sentence paragraph will have precisely five sentences in the prescribed form.

We believe, instead, that no single structure could possibly accommodate the wide variety of shapes human thought can take.
The five-part Topic Sentence paragraph fails for many reasons, including:

1. It assumes that the issue of the paragraph can always be stated in a single sentence.
2. It assumes that the issue of the paragraph is the same as its point
3. It limits our perspective on the paragraph as a structure; it treats a paragraph as a unit that exists all by itself. This distorts the normal experience of a reader, who rarely reads paragraphs in isolation.

To improve our paragraphs, let us take a closer look at the relationship between the issue of a paragraph and the point it tries to make.

### The Issue

The Topic Sentence was right about one important aspect of reading: it is generally the case that readers expect a paragraph will be about whatever appears up front.
In this sense, a paragraph resembles a sentence: whatever comes first provides a context for what follows.
In contrast with the Topic Sentence approach, paragraphs written by skillful, mature writers _unfold_.
We read each succeeding sentence of a paragraph to see how that issue develops.

In well-written paragraphs, the issue tackled in the paragraph will be provided by its first few sentences.
Consider this paragraph:

> Much has happened since the Apollo program and the Mercury and Gemini missions that paved the way for it in the early 1960s.
> Numerous scientific and communications satellites have been launched into Earth orbit.
> Unmanned deep-space probes have been sent to the sun, the moon, and the planets, where they gathered a wealth of information about our solar system.
> Skylab demonstrated that American astronauts could live and work in space for months at a time.
> And a dramatically different launch vehicle entered service: the space shuttle.
> The winged reusable craft was supposed to make space flight routine and cheap.

Notice how the last sentence is crafted here to serve as the sentence that serves the role of the "Herald Stress position" for the next paragraph.
The verb "supposed" introduces uncertainty, and the stress position of that sentence is "cheap".
This paragraph is not in Topic Sentence format, but it effectively leads readers to expect that the following paragraph will be about economic shortcomings of the Space Shuttle era.

### The point

The _issue_ of a paragraph refers to the intellectual geographic boundaries within which the discussion might wander; the _point_ is the interesting place within those boundaries at which the reader is intended to arrive.
In the Topic Sentence model, the issue and the point are forced to be identical; in skillfully articulated prose, this seldom happens.
But what is it that actually does happen, then?
Compactly: readers expect that the point of a paragraph will be stated in a Point sentence, and they have expectations about where in the paragraph to look for it.
In that paragraph above, readers are likely to believe that the last sentence is the Point sentence, especially if the paragraph is followed by a paragraph articulating the economic failures of the Space Shuttle program.

#### In paragraphs with one-sentence issues, readers expect that the point of a paragraph will appear as the last sentence of the paragraph.

#### In paragraphs with multiple-sentence issues, readers expect that the point of a paragraph will appear as the last sentence of the issue.
